Thumbs on Glass

Five steno keyboard ideas, priced by one physical model of thumb motion before any of them is built.

source of truth: android/…/Motor.kt · docs/motor.md · this page runs a JS port of it, live

The model is one mechanism

A motion is a path with tolerances: a list of gates, each a point and the lateral slack that still passes. The model synthesises the speed a thumb would take along that path, and every output is a reading off that one profile. Speed at each point is the lowest of four ceilings, and each ceiling is a named piece of motor physics:

Top speed
v ≤ vMax, under aMax
A thumb is a mass. It cannot step-change its speed.
Curvature
v = bend / ∛κ
The two-thirds power law. Gentle curves stay fast, corners do not.
Tolerance
v so that z·σ ≤ slack
The steering law. A tight channel is threaded slowly.
Homing
v ≤ (dist + slack) / home
Closing on a landing. Integrated, this is Fitts' logarithm.

σ = jitter + noise·v + drift·√glide

That one line ties speed to accuracy. Scatter grows with speed, so the same channel is missed more when it is crossed faster. The drift term is the glass itself: a physical key gives the finger an edge to feel, a slide on glass feels nothing. So a glide runs open loop and wanders like a random walk, and only a touch-down re-anchors it. Taps and swipes are priced by different physics because they are different physics.

A sharp corner is paid separately, as a fresh muscle burst. That is what makes a squiggle dear: not its length, its corners. Time, miss chance, and the comfort components (travel, turning, reach, corners) are then three readings off the same profile.

Five boards, same chords

Every board is asked for the same strokes, from the same rest point, with the same thumb. Cell pitch is 8.7 mm, hard gates get half of that as slack. The flight from rest to the first key, the touch, the glide, and the lift are all in the price.

The separability dial

The weave is swype given a board of its own: the steno order laid on a zigzag, so a skipped key always bends the shape. It has one knob, the amplitude of the zigzag, and the model prices both sides of it. Amplitude buys shape separation linearly. It pays in corners on the chords that walk the weave. And there is a floor neither side can move: a glide at swipe speed carries 4 to 5 mm of scatter, so shape alone cannot finish the job at phone scale. The dictionary stays load-bearing, and pricing that needs a recognizer model beside this motor one.

Projections of the comb

Take-on-entry needs cells, and cells survive any smooth remap of the lattice. So the shipped comb's geometry is a free variable: the same walk, the same order, the same dictionary, drawn on different glass. The model prices two remaps of it here, and they answer differently.

Squeeze fails. Compressing the column axis flattens the chain's zigzag, and the corner tax really does fall. But every millimetre of compression narrows the channel between rival cells, and the steering law charges more for lost tolerance than the corners give back: time and miss rise the deeper the squeeze goes, with no minimum short of the shipped pitch. At a fixed field width the hexagon was already the answer. It is the packing that buys the most slack per glass.

Bend wins. Wrapping the columns onto arcs around the thumb's hinge leaves every distance the walk cares about almost unchanged, so time and miss barely move. What moves is the thumb: walking the order becomes a sweep at constant reach, and radial work drops by two thirds, which is a fifth of the total effort. The shipped tilt setting is the linear term of this remap. The sweep is what tilt is trying to be.

What the model says

  1. The air dominates short strokes. A lone tap costs about 485 ms on every board, and roughly 400 of that is the flight before the glass. Fewer, longer strokes beat many taps, which is the argument for chording on glass at all.
  2. The comb's own walk carries a corner tax. STK zigzags between two columns, so the common case pays 0.6 of a full reversal. Any redesign that straightens the chain pays for itself before it does anything clever.
  3. Swype is a property of the board, not of the finger. On the comb the chain is nearly collinear, so KR and KWR are the same line: a coin flip before the dictionary speaks. Bend the order into a weave and every skip-pair separates, at a price in corners the dial below the boards shows. The hard floor is scatter: 4 to 5 mm of sigma at glide speed, so shape alone cannot finish the job at phone scale and the recognizer's prior must be modeled before swype can be fairly ranked.
  4. The negative key roughly ties the detour it replaces. On KR it removes the corner, and then gives the win back as one more gate and 2 mm more glide: 750 ms against the detour's 743, with more miss. Negatives should be bought where the detour is long or blocked, not wherever a skip exists.
  5. Rays wins exactly where it straightens and shortens. KR becomes one 8.7 mm glide: 551 ms, 3% miss, the best comfort on the page. Where the comb was already straight (SKWR) it buys nothing. So duplicates are a targeted instrument, and the model can rank which chords deserve one.
  6. The projection is a free variable, and bending beats squeezing. Cells survive any smooth remap, so the comb's shape is a choice, not a given. Compressing the columns to kill the zigzag loses: the steering law charges more for the narrowed channel than the corners repay, so the hex pitch was already right. Wrapping the columns around the thumb's hinge wins: the same times, and a fifth of the effort gone. Tilt was the first-order version of this all along.
  7. Drift taxes long glides on every board. A straight SKWR misses 12% at this slack because the thumb has run 26 mm open loop. A mid-stroke re-anchor (lift, touch) is a priceable alternative the model can already compare.

The ranking

All four chords summed, at the committed defaults, one thumb. The order is a judgment the numbers inform, not a sort key: swype on the comb posts the fastest total and still comes last, because its miss column is structural on that board where every other column on the page is tunable.

The ledger

BoardWhat was assumed
Comb (shipped)Take-on-entry walk over the shipped left bank. Hard gates at half the cell pitch. KR detours through open ground east of W. Portals exist and would beat the detour on longer skips; this page prices the walk.
ProjectionsRemaps of the shipped comb, same cells and same walk. Squeeze scales the column axis and scales the slack with the narrowed neighbour distance. The sweep wraps columns onto arcs around the hinge at radius 45 mm, with slack scaled by the local arc compression. Rest point and hand fixed for both; neither re-centres the bent band in the field.
SwypeThe same board, read softly: the recognizer decides, not the cell, so interior gates carry no slack. Accuracy is shape confusion against the nearest rival chord, before any dictionary context. The dictionary would rescue some of it; that rescue is not priced.
Duplicates + negativesThe comb plus an unpress cell placed past R, so KR is glide K→W→R→~W. Where the proposal names no change, the comb's path is used.
Weave (swype's own board)The steno order on a period-4 zigzag chosen so no three keys of a rising run are collinear, which is the property soft recognition needs. Soft gates, 7.5 mm pitch, amplitude 6 mm in the comparison; the dial sweeps it. Only the walk prefix is laid out; vowels and floats await a full design.
Rays (this report's idea)Touch the first key, then one straight slide; duplicate keys sit along the ray so crossing them in order spells the chord. Hard gates at the same 4.3 mm. What it spends is board area and learning, and neither is priced here.

Global caveats: one left thumb, a fixed rest point, parameters are literature-shaped defaults awaiting calibration from recorded tapes, and the model omits practice, visual search, and two-thumb overlap.
