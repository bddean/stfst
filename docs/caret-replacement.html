stfst · two-syllable strokes

Writing the other vowel without XOR

stfst writes a two-syllable word as one stroke marked ^, with the weak vowel XORed into the stressed vowel's chord. The XOR is hard to learn and very hard to read back. This study scores replacements on stfst's real theory and word set, treats every unsystematic fallback outline as something to memorize, and compares new layouts built from scratch.

What one stroke holds

The recommended rule keeps everything stfst writes today and moves the weak vowel off the vowel keys, onto the two keys beside S.

Other vowel^+The weak vowel's first letter: i or y. Keys ^ and +.
OnsetHEvery consonant before the stressed vowel. Left bank.
Stressed vowelAIts chord by spelling, as Typey Type writes it. Unchanged.
Coda-PEvery consonant after it. Right bank, with -S, -D, -G still folding.

The word is happy: ^+HAP. Today it is ^HAEUP, which reads as a long a.

Why ^ is hard to read

  • The vowel keys lie. XOR by any operand maps the 16 vowel chords onto themselves, so each result has four or five parses. today is ^TKAOEU, which reads as tie; always is ^ULS; city is ^S-T, with no vowel at all.
  • Position is gone. report (weak vowel before the stress) and happy (after it) are marked the same way.
  • ^ means several things. XOR with a vowel, an r-coloured vowel absorbed into -R, or only "the plain stroke is taken" (matter is ^PHART because mart is PHART).
  • Undoing it needs the spelling and the vowel table. The reader must guess the stressed chord, guess the operand, and XOR them back, in their head.

Readability

Readability is scored on the stroke as written, without the dictionary, weighted by frequency over the marked words.

  • Readings: how many (stressed chord, other vowel's letter, side of the stress) triples the word set uses that the rule writes with the same marker and vowel keys. Fewer is better. About 4 is the floor on two marker keys.
  • Stressed readings: how many stressed chords those triples have. A rule that leaves the vowel keys alone scores 1.
  • Literal vowel: the share of strokes whose vowel keys are exactly the stressed vowel's chord.
  • One reading: the share of strokes with a single (stressed chord, letter) reading, whatever the side.
  • Memorized: outlines a learner must learn word by word, because no rule produces them. See the fallback study.

Drop-in replacements

resolved · 12,208

Each rule keeps stfst's onset, vowel and coda chords and changes only what the marker keys say. Each is run through stfst's real two-syllable pipeline. Lost is the frequency share of the words that end up with no stroke. Words lost counts words that lose to another word plus words whose outline another dictionary keeps. Every placed word is one stroke.

The two-key ceiling is about 1.44%. A free search over every (side, letter) cell on ^ and + reaches 1.466%. Adding # as a third key only reaches 1.441%, since the stack holds most # strokes. The letter rule gets within 0.11 points of the ceiling with three entries a learner can say aloud. Showing the side of the stress costs much more. Two keys cannot show both the side and the letter, and the letter is worth more.

OR'd marker chords

resolved · 12,208

Here the marker chord may also hold vowel keys or *, OR'd into the stressed chord. The question is whether that buys loss without bringing back XOR's problem.

  • A single ^ with the letter OR'd repeats XOR's problem. or-letter loses 2.471%, more than XOR's 1.824%. happy is ^HAEUP and today ^TKAOEU, the same keys as XOR. In 36% of strokes the weak key is already in the stressed chord, so it is hidden: city is ^SEUT. Searching the whole single-^ family reaches only 1.569%, at 9.41 readings.
  • * for i or y helps, but * is part of stressed chords too. or-side-letter-star, the only design that shows both the side and the letter, loses 1.634%. Its vowel keys are literal in only 34.5% of strokes.
  • OR works best as a small addition to the letter rule. or-plus adds +O and +U to the letter rule. It loses 1.340% alone, 0.234 points less. The OR'd keys are rare letters, so only 6.5% of strokes hide the weak key. But o's O merges into stressed chords that hold A: also +AOLS, today +TKAOEU, minor +PHAOEURP. Stressed readings rise from 1.00 to 1.43.
  • The ceiling is about 0.95%, with any of 21 OR chords per (side, letter) cell. With one readable chord per letter it is 1.09 to 1.11%. Neither table follows a pattern, and twelve random starts of the second land between 1.088% and 1.161%, so no table stands out.

The fallback is memorization

resolved + fallback · 12,208

A word that loses its one stroke needs some other outline. The study tried three fallbacks:

  • Typey Type: the word's Typey Type outlines, restored from typey-superseded.json.
  • Theory split: the two strokes stfst's theory already writes in theory.tsv, before contract.xfst joins them. Every word gets one by rule.
  • Theory, then Typey Type: the theory's two strokes first, and Typey Type only for the words the theory cannot place.

A Typey Type outline counts as predicted when it is exactly one of the theory's two-stroke outlines. Every other Typey Type outline is unsystematic, so it counts as memorized. That includes one-stroke briefs (actual STAOUL, animal TPHAPBL, prevent PRAOEPBLT) and multi-stroke outlines that follow no rule (favourite TPA*EUFRT/A*U, successfully SUBG/SES/TPHREU).

With Typey Type as the fallback, roughly half the fallback words are memorized under every rule. The count grows with the rule's loss: mark only needs 578 memorized words, the letter rule 295. A Typey Type fallback does not level the rules. It turns their losses into memorization.

What the Typey Type fallback holds, letter rule

With the theory first, 29 words are still left for Typey Type under the letter rule (0.090% of frequency). Most are near pairs whose theory outline the pair's other word holds: referred/preferred, complain/explain, assure/ensure, expel/excel, revolve/resolve, compute/commute. Five words are lost under every fallback: focussed, desserts, methodologies, moderators and gentiles. These are spelling variants and near-homographs.

The theory fallback costs 0.006 strokes per word against Typey Type (1.015 against 1.009). The difference is Typey Type's 113 one-stroke briefs, which the theory writes in two strokes.

De novo foot-zone layouts

hard by sound · 11,344

The foot-zone layouts from the handoff study, rebuilt on stfst's words. Every word is written by sound from lexicon.lexc, and the chords are chosen by search inside zones. A free layout replaces all of stfst's chords. The Ireland rows keep stfst's onset, coda and vowel chords and search only the weak-syllable markers.

A layout by sound has one outline per word, so these rows are scored by hard collision with no stack. The drop-in rules are scored the same way here, which is why their numbers are higher than in the resolved tables. Homophones stay apart, which puts a floor of 0.51% under any layout by sound (whether/weather, principal/principle, center/centre).

The free foot-zone layouts sit almost on the homophone floor. Nearly every word they lose is a homophone or spelling pair. But they replace every chord stfst has, with search-chosen chords, and they give up spelling, which separates many homophones in stfst. Kept to stfst's own chords and keys, the by-sound Ireland layout does worse than stfst's spelling theory: 4.32% against 2.80% for the letter rule on the same two keys.

For context, on the handoff's own 78,853 words hard · 78,853 · handoff its layout A loses 0.10% at 6.73 keys per stroke. This study's solver reproduces that and finds a layout at the same loss with 6.49 keys per stroke. Those numbers are not comparable with the rows above.

A plain de novo board

hard by sound · 11,344

This asks what a key chart built from scratch is worth under plain standard theory: one stroke per syllable, onset on the left bank, vowel on the thumbs, coda on the right bank. The board is stfst's own: 8 left keys (S1 and S2 apart), *, the four thumbs and 10 right keys, with no ^ + @ #. The vowels keep Ireland's chords by sound. Each consonant gets its own chord on each bank, with at most 3 keys, and the search also rewards clusters written in steno order.

Each key shows the consonant it writes alone, above its position. Steno order on the left is s k p m t n r l. On the right it is r n k l m p s t d v. Other consonants are two-key chords: on the left b KP, d TP, g TW, f SP, h TK; on the right g -PB, b -RB, f -GT, ng -FR. -ed is -D and -s is -Z.

  • Under plain theory, Ireland's chart loses almost nothing. It loses 0.60%, 0.09 points above the homophone floor. The searched board reaches the floor. With * as the disambiguator both lose 0.00%.
  • The board gains effort and order. 3.56 weighted keys per stroke against 4.12 (14% less), and 99% of clusters in steno order against 92% of onsets and 84% of codas. Ordering the clusters costs almost nothing: 3.56 keys per stroke at 99% in order, against 3.48 when order is not rewarded and only 22% of onsets are in order.
  • The plain theory buys its low loss with 2.4 strokes per word. The word set has almost no one-syllable words, so a stroke per syllable never meets its real rivals. 0.51% is optimistic for that reason.

Board or theory

The same three theories, run once on Ireland's chart and once on a searched chart. Only the consonant chords and key order change.

The theory dominates. Going from one stroke per word to one per syllable removes 3.72 points on Ireland's board. Changing the board removes at most 1.62 points, and only in the most contracted theory. There Ireland's place chords nest (T ⊂ TP ⊂ TPH, P ⊂ PH), so unions of whole-word clusters erase consonants. Even searched, contracted strokes write only 50 to 60% of their clusters in order, because clusters that span a syllable boundary cannot all be ordered on one bank.

A left-hand voicing key

resolved · 12,208

The idea is one key that voices a left-bank consonant: d +T, b +P, g +K, v +TP, z +S, j +KH. th/dh and sh/zh keep one chord each. The chords Ireland spends on voiced consonants (TK PW TKPW SR STKPW SKWR) would then be free.

Verdict: not worth it as stated.

  • No key is free. # is the number bar and phrasing's gate (#T is already "2"). ^ is the two-syllable marker. @ is Emily's modifiers. Only + could be taken, and the letter rule needs it.
  • On + it is worth less than the letter rule. The best voicing rule on +, with today's XOR, loses 1.710%. The letter rule alone loses 1.571%.
  • On a key of its own (@ standing in, with Emily's outlines dropped) it adds to the letter rule: 1.571% to 1.453%, and 43 words gain a one-stroke outline. With the theory fallback, strokes per word go from 1.015 to 1.014.
  • Retraining is large. 38,516 of Typey Type's 132,060 entries change (29.2%), and 13.3% of running-text words get a new outline. b and d go from one finger to two.
  • Ireland already encodes voicing. In one-syllable words the key is a one-to-one re-encoding, so no dictionary gains or loses a collision. The gain comes only from consonant unions in contracted strokes.

The follow-up worth doing is v alone. Changing only v's chord, from SR to +TP, gives two-thirds of the gain: −0.073 points with XOR and −0.071 with the letter rule, out of −0.110 and −0.118 for full voicing. SR's keys are the commonest consonants before a stressed syllable (re-, s-), so v in a contracted stroke reads as re- or s-. Options are v as TP in contracted strokes only, a v chord that avoids S and R, or the key for v only. 463 one-stroke Typey Type entries sit on SR.

Six vowel keys

hard · 3,606 · approx.

The design is in ~/.config/plover/VOWELS-DESIGN.md §10, "six thumb keys". Each thumb has a row of three keys and reaches 6 states, so the thumbs have 36 vowel states against 16 for AOEU. In a two-syllable stroke the left thumb spells the first syllable's vowel letter and the right thumb the second's. It was evaluated there and not built.

A cheap score: the 3,606 marked words with exactly two vowels, the stfst base stroke's consonants, each syllable's first vowel letter on its thumb, every stroke marked. Hard collision among these words only.

It does no better than the letter rule. It drops the stressed vowel's long or short digraph, and it needs a new board, so Typey Type and the rest of the stack would not carry over. A fair score would need a new system and theory. There is no layout file for it.

  • Loss. Alone the letter rule loses 1.574% (589 words, 66 of zipf above 4), against 1.824% for ^. With the theory split behind it, 0.093% (34 words, 4 of zipf above 4) at 1.015 strokes per word.
  • Memorization. Nothing to memorize beyond the rule and stfst's theory. A Typey Type fallback would add 295 word-by-word outlines. Keeping Typey Type only after the theory adds 29 and brings the loss to 0.003%.
  • Readability. 3 marker states to learn instead of an XOR table. The vowel keys are always literal (1.00 stressed readings, against 5.45), and readings fall from 10.4 to 4.2. ^ alone means "a weak e, or none", one meaning instead of three.
  • It stays inside stfst's theory. The operand is still the first letter by spelling, the vowel chord still comes from typey-vowels.toml, and inflection folds and -GS, -ment and the other rewrites are untouched. Only caret.xfst's XOR becomes a table from operand to marker, and the losers take their theory.tsv outlines.

Runners-up

  • or-plus (the letter rule, with o as +O and u as +U). 1.340% alone, 0.234 points less. With the theory fallback both lose 0.093%, and or-plus needs 1.012 strokes per word against 1.015. The cost is literal vowel keys: 86.5% of strokes instead of 100%, and today becomes +TKAOEU.
  • Side and y (^ after the stress, + before it, ^+ a weak i or y after it). The only rule that shows position. 2.397% alone. With the theory fallback, 0.119% at 1.023 strokes per word.
  • Mark only (^ means two syllables, and nothing else). One rule. 4.061% alone. With the theory fallback, 0.132% at 1.039 strokes per word, 1,246 words on two strokes, nothing memorized. Choose it if one key with one meaning is worth putting 2.4 points more of the frequency on two strokes.
  • The searched table on ^ + (stfst-side-free.toml). 1.466% alone, the best on two keys without OR, but its 12 cells follow no pattern. It is a ceiling, not a rule.

Open decisions

  • The 29 words the theory cannot place. Keep their Typey Type outlines as memorized entries (0.003% lost), or drop them (0.093% lost). Most are near pairs such as referred/preferred and assure/ensure.
  • Word boundaries in the fallback. A two-stroke outline whose strokes are each words (HAP/PEU) is the usual steno word-boundary risk. Typey Type outlines carry the same risk.
  • Letter or or-plus. or-plus loses 0.23 points less alone. With the theory fallback the two differ by 0.002 strokes per word, and the letter rule keeps the vowel keys literal.
  • Letter or side. The letter rule loses less. The side rule says where the weak syllable is. Both fit on ^ and +; neither fits with the other.
  • The other users of ^ and +. Two-word pairs (two_word.py, join.xfst) and affix stems also end in caret.xfst. Folds ("a X", "X of", "X is", "X to") use ^, and phrasing-tag uses #^ and ^+. The scores count every outline the stack holds now, but the two-word dictionary was not rerun under the new rule.
  • A weak e and no vowel share ^. Giving the bare mark (append, mother) a chord of its own costs about 0.1 points (1.678% with ^+).
  • Spelling variants. focussed/focused and desserts/deserts count as losses under every rule. They could be dropped from scoring.
  • A v chord. v's SR reads as re- or s- in contracted strokes. Fixing v alone is the part of the voicing idea worth studying.
  • Words of 3+ syllables. 10,115 lexicon words, 3.1% of tokens, sit outside the two-syllable dictionary. They would need a skeleton theory of their own. This study does not score one.

Running the solver

The solver is the stfst-layout crate. Each candidate is a TOML file in layouts/. Words are encoded by scripts/layout-units.py and cached under build/layout. A run writes build/layout/<design>.json with the score, chords, losers and fallbacks. The two-syllable source needs a compiled stack (build/dicts) and the pipeline TSVs (build/work/two-syllable/pipeline). It does not recompile the stack.

cargo build --release -p stfst-layout
./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/stfst-letter.toml           # resolved against the stack, alone
./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/stfst-letter-split.toml     # with the theory's two strokes behind it
./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/stfst-letter-theory.toml    # theory, then Typey Type
./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/stfst-letter-typey.toml     # Typey Type behind it
./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/stfst-or-plus.toml          # OR'd: +O and +U
./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/stfst-or-free-own.toml      # descent over [choices]
./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/stfst-letter-voice-pretonic.toml
./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/stfst-letter.toml --hard --eval    # hard collision, no stack
./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/novo-plain.toml             # plain de novo board, by sound
FOOT_STENO=<handoff dir> ./target/release/stfst-layout layouts/novo-a.toml   # foot zones, by sound
  • --eval scores the start chords only. --warm RESULT.json starts from a result's chords. --moves, --seeds, --method anneal|descent|anneal+descent and -o override [search].
  • encode.form = "position" gives each marked stroke an atom X:a:E or X:b:E (the side of the stress, then the operand), and "operand" gives X:E. [fixed] pins a rule, [zones] lets search choose within keys, and [choices] limits an atom to a list of chords.
  • encode.fallback is "typey", "theory", or a list of them in order. The run prints how many words land on each.
  • encode.reach lists the keys, or the chords, whose outlines are asked of the stack. It must cover every marker chord, or those outlines are scored as free.
  • encode.voice and encode.bare drive the voicing variants. objective.order and objective.distinct drive the plain board.
  • The first run of a design encodes its words, which takes 40 s to 2 minutes, most of it stack lookups. Later runs read the cache.

Speed

  • Resolve mode. One full resolve of the 12,208 words takes about 1 ms. Descent scores every chord of each free atom in parallel, so the 12 marker cells of a two-key table finish in under 0.1 s.
  • Hard-collision mode. On the handoff's 78,853 words, one annealing move takes about 450 µs on one thread, against 1.5 to 2 ms for the handoff's numba solver (3.5 to 4.5 times faster). Twelve seeds of 300k moves plus descent run in parallel in 13 minutes. The handoff took about 8 minutes for one. On the 11,344 words, a plain-board design of 8 seeds × 300k moves takes about 50 s.
  • What makes it fast. A read-only incremental delta, so a rejected move costs no mutation. Fixed atoms folded into a per-unit base. Words that always collide merged into one unit. Warm starts, parallel seeds and parallel descent candidates. A group index that never needs rebuilding. Encodings cached on disk.
